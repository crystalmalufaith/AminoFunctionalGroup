1. Compound Identification

Compound: Glycine

Chemical Formula: NH2CH2COOH

Glycine, the smallest amino acid, has a NH2 and COOH. In the brain it is a neuropeptide; acting there as a neurotransmitter in addition to being a powerful regulator of other neurotransmitters (such as serotonin).

2. Isomer Comparison

The chemical form of glycine is also partially isomerized to methyl carbamate. For example, glycine with amino and carboxyl groups bonded to the same C-atom[3] is not a PO inhibitor whereas methyl carbamate with these two groups at different C-atoms is.

This structural difference is reflected even more strongly in the chemical reactivity: glycine is easily dissolved by water with a significantly amphoteric but zwitterionic side chain, whereas methyl carbamate behaves like an ester.

3. Polymer Formation

Polymers of glycine As amino acids, glycines can be polymerized to form long chains (polymers or copolymers). One is the amino group of a glycine bonding to the carboxy group of another and so on for peptide linkages (-CONH-). Polyglycine can kink and also turn corners, and it is lightweight to serve as a kind of support frame for proteins such as collagen.

4. Chemical Reactions

2 NH₂CH₂COOH → NH₂CH₂CONHCH₂COOH + H₂O

Another reaction is combustion, producing carbon dioxide, water, and nitrogen:

4 NH₂CH₂COOH + 9 O₂ → 8 CO₂ + 10 H₂O + 2 N₂

5. Preparation and Synthesis

Glycine is produced industrially by Strecker synthesis, and synthesizing it using the frustrated Lewis pair would likely not have any advantages over the current methodology.

In the human body, the enzyme serine hydroxymethyltransferase can transform glycine into serine and generate 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate; while for animals that are incapable of synthesizing it, glycine is one of the essential amino acids.
